How to listen

What this is

The Earth Information Jukebox turns NASA pictures of our planet into music you can listen to and explore. It is built for blind and low-vision listeners, and for everyone else.

How to start

  1. Choose a dataset from the list.
  2. Press Play. Sound only starts after you press it.
  3. Use headphones if you can: sounds move between your left and right ear.

What you hear

  • Pitch: higher notes mean more of what the record measures, such as more city light or more lightning. In the sea temperature record the melody follows the seasons.
  • Loudness and brightness of the sound follow how bright or busy the picture is.
  • Left or right tells you where on the picture things are happening.
  • Drum hits in the lightning record are storms bursting.

To hear the scale first, press Audio legend (or G): it plays the notes from the lowest to the highest value.

Explore the picture

Press E or the Explore button, then use the arrow keys to move a sound cursor over the picture. Each step plays the value under the cursor and says where you are. W repeats where you are, C compares the spot with the average, and Escape takes you back to Play. A soft tick means there is no data in that spot.

Narration

Under Sound settings, choose Off, Brief or Detailed. While a record plays, your screen reader (such as VoiceOver, NVDA or JAWS) reads a short description every few seconds when something changes. The same words appear under the video for sighted listeners.

The four records

  • Sea surface temperature: one year of ocean currents and water temperature, March 2007 to March 2008. It shows the seasons and the currents, not a climate trend.
  • Night lights: city lights from 2012 to 2023. We re-paced NASA's video to one year every 2.5 seconds so the music can follow.
  • Lightning from the space station: about 2017 to 2023, with no exact dates. The station can't see beyond about 52 degrees north or south.
  • Carbon dioxide: a tour around a turning globe, January to March 2020. The sound follows the part of the globe facing you, and values are relative: the colour scale has no units.
