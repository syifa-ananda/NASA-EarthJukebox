How to listen

The Earth Jukebox plays NASA pictures of our planet as music. This page explains how to start, what the sounds mean and how to explore a picture by ear, with a keyboard, a screen reader or a mouse.

← Back to the jukebox

What this is

The Earth Information Jukebox turns NASA pictures of our planet into music you can listen to and explore. It is built for blind and low-vision listeners, and for everyone else.

Each dataset is a short NASA video. The music is made live from the picture, frame by frame, so what you hear always matches what is on screen.

How to start

  1. Choose a dataset from the list.
  2. Press Play (or K). Sound only starts after you press it.
  3. The first time you play Sea Surface Temperature, its recorded violin and cello take a moment to load, usually under a second and at most about 8 seconds. The picture starts at once and the music joins when they are ready. If they can't load, synthesised strings play instead.

Headphones help: sounds move between your left and right ear, so you can hear where things happen.

What you hear

  • Pitch: higher notes mean more of what the dataset measures, such as more city light or more lightning. In the sea temperature dataset the melody follows the seasons.
  • Loudness and brightness of the sound follow how bright or busy the picture is.
  • Left or right tells you where on the picture things are happening.
  • The instruments are different for each dataset: see The four datasets.
  • A soft wordless voice in the carbon dioxide dataset marks the moments when the most carbon dioxide is in view. This is the climate alarm. Under Sound settings you can switch it off, or press Hear the alarm for a short example.

To hear the scale first, press Audio legend (or G): it plays the notes from the lowest to the highest value and says what each sound means.

Explore the picture

Press E or the Explore button, then use the arrow keys to move a sound cursor over the picture. Each step plays the value under the cursor and says where you are. W repeats where you are, C compares the spot with the average, and Escape takes you back to Play. A soft tick means there is no data in that spot.

You can also point at the picture with a mouse or a finger.

VoiceOver: turn Quick Nav off first (press Left arrow and Right arrow together), so the arrow keys reach the sound map.

Narration

Under Sound settings, choose Off, Brief or Detailed (or press N). While a dataset plays, your screen reader (such as VoiceOver, NVDA or JAWS) reads a short description every few seconds when something changes. The same words appear under the video for sighted listeners.

Press D at any time for a detailed description of the moment you are on.

The four datasets

  • Sea surface temperature: one year of ocean currents and water temperature, March 2007 to March 2008. Slow strings (a recorded violin and cello) and a soft wordless choir follow the seasons, with a few harp notes on top. It shows the seasons and the currents, not a climate trend.
  • Night lights: city lights from 2012 to 2023. Bamboo angklung and harp notes, over a wooden marimba bass, fill in as the lights grow, and a low angklung chord marks each new year. We re-paced NASA's video to one year every 2.5 seconds so the music can follow.
  • Lightning from the space station: about 2017 to 2023, with no exact dates. Each storm burst is a hand-drum stroke, heard where it happens, over a soft wordless choir. The station can't see beyond about 52 degrees north or south.
  • Carbon dioxide: a tour around a turning globe, January to March 2020. A low overtone drone and a slow drum heartbeat follow the part of the globe facing you; the heartbeat quickens when more carbon dioxide is in view. Values are relative: the colour scale has no units.
